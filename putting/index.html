How the Putting Model Works

A ball is rolled across a 3D survey of each green to find the line and pace that hole a putt. Those results feed two statistical models, fitted on the 2026 season's putts: the chance of one putt, and the chance of three or more. Six steps, in the order they run.

312,183 putts 558 greens 31 courses 2,166 hole locations

1SURFACE

Start with the real green

Each green is a surveyed mesh of triangles with points about 35 centimetres apart. It is sampled onto a grid of heights every 25 centimetres. Heights and slopes between grid points are interpolated with smooth (bicubic) curves, so the slope changes continuously. No smoothing is applied.

Ball and hole positions come from the tour's shot tracking, which uses a different coordinate system from the green surveys. Each course gets a fitted rotation and shift to align the two. No scaling is applied.

Alignment error

Average vertical gap between tracked positions and the surveyed surface, per course: 1.3 to 10.7 millimetres.

2SOLVE

Find the line that holes it

The ball's path is simulated in 5 millisecond steps. Slope pushes it sideways and rolling friction slows it. Friction is set from the green speed (Stimpmeter reading) measured at each tournament. The ball only drops if it reaches the hole slowly enough: at most 1.31 metres per second through the centre, less off-centre.

For each putt the solver finds:

Pacespeed that would finish 40 cm past the hole
Lineaim that holes it at that pace
Windowrange of aims that still hole it, in degrees

Aims are searched up to 60 degrees either side of straight.

3HOLING

Will it go in?

The solver's outputs are inputs to a statistical model (a generalised additive model, which fits a flexible curve for each input) predicting whether each of the season's putts was holed. It is fitted on the 310,623 putts where the solver found a line.

Inputs

Distance and window enter as one combined surface, so the effect of the window can differ by distance. The model also includes adjustments for each green, hole location and tournament; these are left out of the maps.

Inputs are fitted as curves, so there is no single coefficient. Shape is the effective degrees of freedom: 1 is a straight line, higher is more curved. Strength is the chi-squared test statistic. Evidence summarises its p-value.

Performance

Tested on tournaments left out of fitting, log-loss (the standard error measure for probability predictions) is 0.27% lower than distance alone. Deviance explained: 57.8%.

4MISSING

And if it misses?

The second model is fitted on the 118,351 putts that missed. It predicts whether three or more putts were still needed from that position.

Each putt is re-simulated at 5% firmer and 5% softer than the solved pace, and the distance from the hole where each finishes is an input. (Across 162,331 tracked putts, tour players' median pace error is 9.8%.)

The soft result predicts three-putts; the firm result does not.

5COMBINE

Expected putts

The two models combine into expected putts, counting three or more as three:

Expected = 2 − chance of one + chance of three or more

Map colours

Every map uses the same fixed scale: 0 to 100% for probabilities, 1 to 3 for expected putts. The same colour means the same value on every green.

6NO LINE

When nothing holes it

From some positions no aim within 60 degrees of straight holes the putt at any pace. This covers 4.2% of mapped green area across the season. The highest are TPC San Antonio at 17.7% (holes 2 and 7 share one large green) and TPC Craig Ranch at 9.3%. 1,166 real putts were hit from these positions.

These positions are handled in three steps.

1. Search nearby

The survey resolves about 35 centimetres, so for the maps the solver also tries points up to 35 centimetres away. If any of them has a line, that line is used.

2. Lay up

Otherwise the solver finds the aim and pace that leave the ball closest to the hole, also trying 90% of that pace. The lay-up is simulated at five pace errors spanning the tour distribution (standard deviation 14.7%), and the one-putt model is applied at each finishing point. The results are weighted into:

Chance of three or more1 − average chance of holing the second putt
Runs off the greenshare of lay-ups leaving the surveyed surface

Lay-ups that leave the green are excluded from the average and reported separately, since the model cannot score a putt from the fringe. A lay-up that drops counts as holing the second putt.

3. Apply a minimum

The chance of three or more is never set below that of a missed putt of the same length, from a distance-only version of the three-putt model: 0.8% at 1.8 metres, 1.7% at 6 metres, 19.6% at 18 metres. Without it, a no-line position could score better than an ordinary putt of the same length.

PLAYERS

Player ability

Adding a term for each player to the one-putt model improves it: log-loss falls 0.34% below distance alone, against 0.27% without players.

The maps leave it out because of compute. They are built from 1.93 million precomputed solves, and a per-player version would repeat them for each of 452 players. The maps show the tour average.

LIMITS

Limitations

No-line positions are pessimistic. From the 1,166 real putts, players holed 5.0% and took three or more 23.2% of the time; the model gives about 56%. The survey ends at the green's edge, so putting via the fringe is not modelled.

Long putts are extrapolated. 99.87% of putts were under 25 metres. Maps cover the whole green, including the far side of greens shared by two holes.

Two events use an assumed green speed. They have no tracking data, so the season median is used and the page is marked "assumed".

Detail under 35 centimetres is not captured, nor are grain, wind or surface wear.
